My story

A question that kept growing.

Humans have survived war, disease, disaster, loss, and suffering. Yet some of us also experience the periodic desire to hurt ourselves or not exist. Understanding that paradox has become central to everything I do. Here is how I got here.

01
Pune, India
2015 – 2020

Curiosity

Research found me during my undergraduate years in Psychology and Law. Learning how to collect and analyze data, and reading peer-reviewed work, sparked a fascination with how data can reveal patterns in the way people function. I built a research proposal on selective attention, movement, and memory, and later contributed to a project on mental healthcare structures in India and the well-being of the professionals who staff them.

Those four years marked the birth of my fascination with data and with the process of academic research.
Threads started: heterogeneitycontext How do people differ, and what shapes the systems around them?
02
Penn State
2020 – 2022

Rigor

To learn what robust empirical research takes, I joined Dr. Michelle Newman's lab as a volunteer research assistant. We studied predictors of and interventions for mood disorders. I helped with data collection and compliance monitoring for a randomized trial of a mindfulness-based ecological momentary intervention, and I saw how EMA could deliver real-time support while capturing what cross-sectional designs can't.

In parallel, I sharpened my analysis skills on trauma data: conceptualizing questions, running models in R and Mplus, and writing and revising the manuscripts. Those projects became my first first-authored papers, on PTSD symptom classes after Hurricane Ike and on self-acceptance as a link between childhood abuse and adult depression and anxiety.

Threads started: dynamicsheterogeneity What can changes within a person tell us that a single snapshot can't?
03
Columbia
2021 – 2024

Questions that scale

My master's at Teachers College opened up opportunity after opportunity. In Dr. George Bonanno's lab I studied how emotion regulation, flexibility, and resilience differ between civilians and Veterans, using EMA, prospective designs, meta-analysis, and machine learning. I co-authored a meta-analysis of emotion regulation and mental health across cultures, which made me ask when results hold across populations and when they don't.

To see a study through from start to finish, I led my own, funded by a Dean's Grant for Student Research. It compared components of emotion regulation flexibility in young adults with and without self-injurious behavior.

In Dr. Christine Cha's lab I led the data monitoring team for an EMA study of suicidal ideation and future thinking in adolescents, and conducted risk assessments and safety plans. The ambiguity in deciding who counts as "suicidal" pushed me to think harder about how risk changes and how we measure it. My thesis used natural language processing in Python to compare future thinking in suicidal and nonsuicidal adolescents, and it led me to ask how machine learning can make sense of passive data like text and facial expression.

I became curious about how to quantify what people show us without asking.
Threads deepened: dynamicsheterogeneity Who is at risk, when, and how would we know?
04
New York & Philadelphia
2022 – 2026

Into the room

A big part of my research meant working with people in acute distress, including those with suicidal ideation and trauma-exposed groups such as Veterans. I wanted the clinical competence to match. As a Sexual Violence Advocate at Columbia Health, I took 16- to 24-hour on-call shifts as a first-tier responder, offering crisis support, safety planning, and connection to resources for survivors in moments of acute need.

I then volunteered on a psychosis unit at NewYork-Presbyterian and joined Dr. Anthony Ahmed's research group. I started with data collection, saw patients one-on-one under supervision, and led groups on the inpatient unit and in the outpatient department. After graduating I moved into a full-time research role, where we used LASSO regression to separate high- and low-risk groups by suicidality and emotion-based impulsivity in patients with psychosis. The question kept returning: how do we identify who is at highest risk, and why?

My training widened from there: inpatient and forensic units at Bellevue, individual psychotherapy at a private practice in Philadelphia, and most recently case management and harm-reduction counseling in supportive housing. Each setting runs differently, serves different people, and demands different skills.

Together these experiences fueled my interest in the nuances of trauma and in brief interventions for stressful life events.
Threads deepened: contextheterogeneity Science that doesn't survive contact with real clinical settings isn't finished.
05
UMass Amherst
2026 – 2032

What's next

I'm now a PhD student in Clinical Psychology at UMass Amherst, working with Dr. Katherine Dixon-Gordon. Her work on borderline personality disorder fits closely with my interest in suicide-related behavior, given the shared, transdiagnostic patterns of risk. I'm especially drawn to her smartphone-based EMA research on dynamic risk processes.

My aim is to examine the dynamic, heterogeneous nature of emotion regulation, self-injurious thoughts and behaviors, and suicide-related behaviors across contexts and over time. I hope to highlight the dynamic risk factors behind adverse outcomes and the protective factors behind adaptive functioning, with the goal of individualized treatments and preventive tools such as just-in-time adaptive interventions. Long term, I want a faculty position at a research-oriented university or center.
