Research

Risk isn't static. Neither is my work.

I study emotion regulation, self-injurious thoughts and behaviors, and suicide-related behaviors across contexts and over time, with a focus on trauma-exposed and vulnerable populations. Three questions organize the program.

01 · Dynamics

How does risk change, and when?

Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies miss what happens in between. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) lets us watch intensity and frequency shift in daily life, which supports an idiographic picture of each person rather than a group average.

This thread started at Penn State with a mindfulness-based EMA intervention trial, grew through EMA studies at Columbia on emotion regulation flexibility and on suicidal adolescents, and is the heart of my PhD with Dr. Dixon-Gordon.

Questions I'm asking

  • How do changes in suicidal ideation unfold within a person, and what predicts them?
  • How flexibly do people adapt their emotion regulation to context, and does flexibility protect?
  • How do interpersonal reactivity and emotion regulation difficulties change over a year in borderline personality disorder?

02 · Heterogeneity

Who is at risk, and why do they differ?

The same exposure or diagnosis produces very different outcomes. Demographics, culture, psychiatric history, and trauma exposure all shape presentation, and group labels like "suicidal" versus "nonsuicidal" can hide as much as they show.

I use person-centered and data-driven methods to find subgroups, trajectories, and cross-population patterns, and to test whether findings hold across diverse samples.

Evidence so far

  • Cross-cultural: meta-analysis of emotion regulation and mental health across cultures (Nature Human Behaviour, 2025).
  • Subgroups: latent class analysis of PTSD symptoms after Hurricane Ike (Social Science & Medicine, 2023).
  • Trajectories: depression trajectories that predict mortality in cancer patients (J. Behavioral Medicine, 2024).
  • Risk classification: LASSO regression on suicidality and impulsivity in psychosis (Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2025).

03 · Context & care

Where does this science have to work?

My research sits next to the people it's about: survivors of violence, patients with psychosis, Veterans, adolescents in crisis, people in supportive housing. Working in crisis response, inpatient and forensic units, outpatient practice, and community settings keeps my questions grounded in how care is actually delivered.

What that looks like

  • Trauma exposure and its long-term impact on functioning, including a study of self-acceptance in the link between childhood abuse and adult symptoms.
  • Future thinking and suicidality in adolescents, using natural language processing on what they say.
  • Brief, individualized interventions for stressful life events.

Where it's heading

From understanding to just-in-time help.

The goal is to pin down dynamic risk factors that drive harm, and protective factors that support adaptive functioning, then use them to build individualized treatment and prevention, including just-in-time adaptive interventions that reach someone when it matters most.
